# Geopolitical Shocks and Green Bond Pricing: Cross-Country Evidence from News and Markets

## Abstract

Does geopolitical stress alter green bond performance uniformly across markets? This study integrates sampled news, contractual verification and paired prices around the February 2026 escalation involving Iran. Across 74 issuer-matched pairs in seven currencies, green quoted prices weaken by approximately 0.092% relative to conventional comparators. Yield-curve conditioning substantially reduces the estimate on identical euro and dollar observations. Documented sovereign pairs reveal different conditional patterns: the Australian decline attenuates after local-rate adjustment, whereas New Zealand exhibits a negative conditional coefficient. A joint model estimates a New Zealand–Australia contrast of −0.020 log percentage points; shared-date and alternative-price tests preserve its direction, with specification-sensitive precision. Reconstruction from published instrument yields supports a clean-price interpretation. The contribution is a directly tested sovereign comparison within an international repricing framework, showing how local financial conditions and valuation conventions shape the interpretation of green bond performance during geopolitical stress.

Keywords: Bond pricing; Geopolitical risk; Green bonds; News sentiment; Sustainable finance.

## 1 Introduction

A bond can finance the energy transition and still lose value when geopolitical tensions intensify. The environmental purpose of its proceeds does not remove its exposure to discount rates, liquidity or contractual cash flows. This distinction becomes especially consequential when conflict changes the outlook for energy supply and financial conditions at the same time. A decline in green bond prices can indicate a change in investors’ environmental preferences, an ordinary fixed-income response, or a combination of both. Separating these possibilities is essential before interpreting market movements as evidence that sustainable finance is being penalised.

The theoretical relationship follows from the interaction between investor preferences and conventional asset pricing. Environmental preferences can affect equilibrium valuations and expected returns (Pástor et al., 2021). Realised performance also reflects unexpected changes in preferences and economic conditions, so a green asset’s return during a particular episode need not reveal its long-run expected return advantage or disadvantage (Pástor et al., 2022). For bonds, contractual features add a further layer. Securities issued by the same borrower can react differently to a common rate movement when their coupons, cash-flow timing or embedded options differ. Geopolitical stress therefore provides an informative setting for studying relative pricing, but does not by itself isolate the value of a green label.

Existing research establishes that environmental preferences, issuer credibility and issue characteristics matter for green bond valuations. Zerbib (2019) uses matched conventional instruments and liquidity adjustments, while Larcker and Watts (2020) emphasise near-identical contractual comparisons in the municipal market. Flammer (2021) and Tang and Zhang (2020) examine issuer and shareholder outcomes. Fornari et al. (2026) consider environmental quality and the green designation. Geopolitical applications include international issuance (Mertzanis & Tebourbi, 2025), matched-bond pricing in China (Lian & Hou, 2024), and international corporate greenium evidence (Fiorillo et al., 2024). Recent work also connects greenium dynamics to investor demand and interest-rate conditions (Pietsch & Salakhova, 2025). The intersection of geopolitical risk and green bond pricing is therefore established; the present question concerns the interpretation of an international security-level response to a dated event.

The gap addressed here concerns how an international sustainable-debt repricing pattern translates into comparisons between financially comparable claims. An international price divergence may combine environmental demand with credit hierarchy, inflation protection, coupon carry and interest-rate exposure. A pooled average alone cannot show whether rate adjustment has similar implications across documented cases. The study addresses this problem by combining a broad matched panel with contractual verification, local-currency yield curves and prices reconstructed from published instrument yields. The comparison asks both whether relative prices change and whether the conditional changes differ across the documented pairs.

This study examines the escalation involving Iran on 28 February 2026. The date is established from an official notification of military action, independently of the bond data (President of the United States, 2026). The analysis combines 14,642 deduplicated news documents sampled from GDELT with a broad LSEG panel of 74 issuer pairs in seven currencies. Documented credit-hierarchy and nominal–inflation-linked mismatches are excluded. Remaining pairs satisfy issuer, currency and maturity conditions; additional contractual evidence determines their suitability for more restrictive tests. The sample includes 44 nationally assigned issuers spanning 21 jurisdictions, three supranational issuers and 27 unresolved country assignments. Country denotes documented legal location or sovereign identity rather than ultimate risk exposure. The study uses an international issuer sample; it does not estimate country-level treatment differences.

Three contributions follow. First, the study documents international relative green bond repricing during a dated geopolitical episode and evaluates its sensitivity to issuers, currencies and observation coverage. Second, it connects contract verification to local financial conditions: the Australian pair’s negative price difference attenuates with its own yield curve, whereas the New Zealand pair displays a negative conditional difference. Their contrast is estimated directly in a joint model rather than inferred from different individual significance levels. Third, methodological triangulation links the information environment, paired market performance and valuation checks. Prices reconstructed from published instrument yields assess whether the conditional comparison depends on the original price construction. The resulting contribution is a comparative interpretation of sustainable-debt performance, with the breadth of the international panel distinguished from the depth of documented sovereign cases.

The research questions are consequently: RQ1, how does the sampled geopolitical news environment change around the event? RQ2, how do green bond prices evolve relative to conventional bonds of the same issuer and currency? RQ3, how do conditional price changes differ across documented sovereign pairs after local-rate adjustment, and does this comparison survive alternative price construction? The questions are evaluated through the three complementary evidence layers specified in Section 3.4. These questions connect the international pattern to its valuation interpretation without treating a single-event before–after contrast as a general causal green-label discount.

## 2 Literature and conceptual framework

### 2.1 Environmental preferences, expected returns and realised prices

The greenium concerns the valuation of environmental attributes relative to financially comparable conventional debt. Preference-based asset pricing allows investors to accept lower expected financial returns in exchange for environmental characteristics (Pástor et al., 2021). Realised performance additionally reflects unexpected changes in preferences and economic conditions (Pástor et al., 2022). A green security can therefore have a favourable valuation level and still underperform during a particular episode. The distinction is central here: a change in a green–conventional price ratio is not a yield-level greenium, and neither quantity alone measures the environmental effectiveness of the financed projects.

Zerbib (2019) combines bond matching and liquidity adjustment, while Larcker and Watts (2020) emphasise comparisons between closely aligned municipal contracts. Their different settings make comparator construction part of the substantive pricing question. Matching on issuer controls the identity of the borrower, but cannot align seniority, indexation, optionality or every cash-flow sensitivity. The present analysis accordingly treats contractual evidence as an input to interpretation rather than assuming that a same-issuer pair identifies an environmental preference premium.

Corporate issuance studies address related but distinct outcomes. Flammer (2021) examines green issuance and subsequent issuer outcomes; Tang and Zhang (2020) study shareholder benefits. Caramichael and Rapp (2024) investigate the corporate green bond issuance premium. These contributions concern financing or issuer responses, whereas the present estimand is the repricing of outstanding securities. Fornari et al. (2026) further distinguish environmental quality from the green designation in issuance pricing. Taken together, these studies motivate separating the label, the issuer and the security: a financing benefit for the borrower does not imply uniform short-window protection for each bondholder.

### 2.2 Geopolitical risk and competing valuation channels

Caldara and Iacoviello (2022) show how news can measure geopolitical risk. This supports observing the information environment independently of bond returns, but does not make any event-specific text sample equivalent to their index. The GDELT corpus used here describes selected Iran- or Israel-related coverage. Its tone is a textual characteristic, not directly observed investor sentiment, a physical supply shock or a national risk measure for each issuer.

Recent research already connects geopolitics with sustainable debt. Mertzanis and Tebourbi (2025) study international green issuance, and Lian and Hou (2024) analyse matched green bond pricing in China. Fiorillo et al. (2024) examine 1,630 corporate green bonds over November 2012–January 2024 and find that geopolitical risk is associated with a stronger greenium, particularly through geopolitical acts rather than threats. Their evidence also points to heterogeneity related to issue and issuer characteristics. Consequently, the present contribution cannot rest on discovering that geopolitical conditions matter for green bonds. It instead concerns the interpretation of an international price response around a specific, independently dated episode.

There are competing channels. Energy-security concerns may strengthen preferences for transition finance, while risk aversion, discount-rate changes and market liquidity can move conventional bond valuations at the same time. Within a pair, unequal duration and convexity transmit a common yield movement into unequal price changes. An apparent green-price penalty can therefore coexist with a persistent yield-level greenium. Conversely, a residual difference after rate conditioning need not be a pure preference effect: liquidity, curve curvature, cash-flow mismatches and unobserved pricing conditions can remain. The analysis uses these alternatives to structure tests, rather than imposing a sign prediction or labelling every unexplained coefficient a greenium.

### 2.3 Market conditions, sovereign heterogeneity and financial comparability

Pietsch and Salakhova (2025) provide particularly relevant evidence on time-varying valuation. Using euro-area matched bonds over 2016–2023, they document a greenium that strengthens and subsequently weakens, with retail holdings and the rising-rate environment helping explain its dynamics. This places investor demand and ordinary financial conditions in the same pricing framework. The current study observes local curves but not comparable investor holdings; it can test conditional price patterns, while the demand interpretation remains a hypothesis for subsequent research.

Sovereign evidence also cautions against treating market breadth as a single economic mechanism. Ando et al. (2024) estimate issuance yield advantages of approximately four basis points in advanced economies and eleven in emerging markets. Nevatia (2026), studying sovereign sustainability instruments across 44 countries, reports contrasting patterns between advanced economies and emerging and developing economies under a different sample and matching design. The variation across these studies underscores the importance of security definitions, market segment and comparator choice. Australia and New Zealand are examined here as documented instrument cases; their difference cannot establish an advanced-versus-emerging-market mechanism.

Liquidity conditioning can alter a pricing result in either direction. Ayaydın Hacıömeroğlu et al. (2026) find that accounting for market-wide illiquidity reveals a municipal green issuance advantage that can be obscured otherwise. Their setting differs from the secondary-market episode examined here, but supplies an important methodological counterpoint: adding a control is not automatically a procedure that removes a spurious green effect. Its economic meaning depends on the outcome, timing and pathway through which the control operates. Local-rate adjustment is therefore presented as a conditional comparison, not a decomposition of the geopolitical effect.

Two recent working papers extend these issues but are distinguished from peer-reviewed evidence. Panizza et al. (2025) examine sovereign and quasi-sovereign matched bonds alongside the legal content of environmental commitments. Their contractual perspective reinforces the distinction between promised financial payments and the credibility of environmental claims. Brichetti et al. (2026) relate sovereign greenium variation to relative green supply and market depth, finding evidence more consistent with persistent market structure than a simple issuance-event response. These mechanisms motivate future holdings, supply and liquidity tests. Neither working paper provides evidence that these mechanisms caused the Australian–New Zealand contrast in this sample.

### 2.4 Research gap and discriminating empirical implications

The remaining question is how a broad event-associated price divergence translates into evidence from financially documented comparisons. Existing work provides substantial evidence about green yield premia, issuance benefits and geopolitical associations. Less directly answered by those estimands is whether the interpretation of a particular international price change survives common-sample market conditioning, explicit contractual screening and an alternative valuation construction. The study connects those operations within one event window, and tests the difference between two conditional case coefficients directly.

Three implications guide the design. First, an event-associated international pattern should be visible in paired market prices and its timing should be disclosed. Second, a financial interpretation should account for comparator terms, local rates and the distinction between quoted price, clean value and total return. Third, an assertion that two cases respond differently should be tested as a coefficient contrast with their contemporaneous covariance retained. News evidence establishes context for these tests; it does not identify a news-to-price mediation pathway. The contribution is thus an integrated valuation comparison with explicit estimands, rather than a claim that a particular sign must confirm or reject sustainable-investment preferences.

## 3 Data and research design

### 3.1 Event window and bond sample

The observation window extends from 14 January to 3 April 2026, covering 45 pre-event and 35 post-event calendar days. The market panel contains 58 observed dates, including 33 before the event. Because 28 February falls on a Saturday, the first observed post-event market date is 2 March. The official event document establishes timing; the paper does not adopt its political assessments as analytical premises.

LSEG daily records supply instrument and issuer identifiers, currency, issue and maturity dates, green classification, and bid and ask prices. Exact duplicate rows and missing identifiers are removed; no conflicting valid instrument-date records were found. Eligibility requires the same issuer identifier and currency, issuance by the start of the window, maturity after its end, a maturity difference no greater than one year, at least 27 pre-event observations, an observed 27 February reference price and at least one post-event observation. There is one pair per issuer. Two documented structural mismatches are excluded: a subordinated Tier 2 note paired with senior covered debt, and a nominal green bond paired with inflation-linked debt. Table 1 summarises the resulting 74-pair panel with 4,207 observations.

The reference universe was retrieved after the event, with conventional queries limited to the first ten records ordered by maturity. An expanded audit of the saved reference pool identifies 500 same-issuer/currency candidates for the original 76 green instruments; 219 also satisfy issue-date, maturity-date and one-year maturity-gap conditions. Only 76 have saved daily histories, one for each original issuer. A scan of 7,909 additional 2026 history files finds no overlap with these 500 candidate or 76 green ISINs. Candidate availability therefore remains distinct from usable price coverage. The study does not claim an exhaustive or historically investable universe. Nationality is never inferred from currencies or ISIN prefixes; explicit sovereign identities, issuer disclosures and unique GLEIF legal-name matches support the supplementary geographical register.

### 3.2 News and public-source supplementation

The news sample uses GDELT Global Knowledge Graph 2.1 archives at 00:00 and 12:00 UTC on each of the 80 calendar days. These are two 15-minute ingestion batches per day, not two complete half-day news collections. Eligible documents belong to the English-language web stream, contain an Iran or Israel location mention, have an empty translation field and contain at least 100 words. URL deduplication produces 14,642 documents, comprising 4,006 pre-event and 10,636 post-event records. Location mentions define relevance broadly and can include incidental references.

The primary news comparisons weight days equally, avoiding automatic overweighting of days with greater archive volume. GDELT’s document tone is averaged within day; the negative-tone share measures the proportion below zero. An oil-related thematic indicator describes the energy context without being interpreted as a measured supply shock. Robustness checks examine the two ingestion slots separately and a balanced set of 48 publishers with at least ten eligible documents in each period. GDELT documentation defines the underlying fields (GDELT Project, 2015).

Public SEC portfolio filings, issuer final terms and debt-management records supplement contractual information. ISIN and maturity are checked before importing coupons, and filing-specific rate units are calibrated against overlapping LSEG records. A uniform screen records cash-flow indexation, coupon type, seniority, collateral, calls, resets, extensions, payment frequency and payment dates. The plain-contract subset requires nominal fixed cash flows, positive coupons no more than 0.50 percentage points apart, compatible documented seniority/security/frequency, and no call, reset or extension. Unknown fields cannot satisfy the screen. Optional instruments remain a separate category: similar current coupons cannot establish equivalent option exposure. Rules are retrospective, and source-access dates are distinguished from original contractual dates.

Euro-area AAA two- and ten-year spot yields come from the ECB; US two- and ten-year par yields come from the US Treasury. Currency-specific coefficients preserve the distinction between these curve constructions. Missing rates are not carried forward. The revised common-sample rate panel contains 64 euro- or US-dollar pairs and 3,615 observations. The baseline and rate-conditioned regressions use exactly the same instrument-date rows, verified by row hashes.

Pair fixed effects absorb persistent price-level differences. The coefficient β compares average paired log-price gaps across the pre- and post-event periods; it is not a one-day announcement return. In the unbalanced panel, each pair receives weight proportional to n_pre × n_post / (n_pre + n_post). It is a common-event before–after contrast within matched pairs, not a treatment effect identified from staggered or untreated event exposure. Calendar-date fixed effects cannot be included alongside the common post indicator because they would absorb it. Issuer-time shocks common to both instruments are reduced by pairing, while differential cash-flow sensitivities and concurrent conditions remain relevant.

Standard errors use two-way issuer and date clustering following the multiway-clustering framework of Cameron et al. (2011), with finite-sample corrections and t reference degrees of freedom equal to the smaller cluster count minus one. This permits within-issuer dependence and contemporaneous dependence across issuers. It does not guarantee protection against every form of persistent common-time dependence. Confidence intervals and changes across specifications therefore receive greater emphasis than a single significance threshold.

Weekly indicators replace the post dummy to describe timing relative to the final pre-event week. A tighter maturity cap, a pre-period placebo, quote-staleness exclusions, a linear trend and a complete-date panel assess sensitivity. Leave-one-issuer-out and leave-one-currency-out estimates assess concentration. Date-aggregated score HAC calculations with 3, 7 and 10 trading-date lags provide a separate time-dependence diagnostic while preserving the pair-fixed-effect coefficient. They are reported alongside, and do not replace, two-way clustered inference. The weekly model’s usual two-way covariance is indefinite. Joint tests and Figure 2 therefore use the positive-definite sum of issuer- and date-cluster covariance components, retaining their overlap. This conservative sensitivity avoids inversion of the indefinite matrix; it does not guarantee coverage outside the original dependence assumptions. Figure 2 uses simulated simultaneous intervals for all eleven coefficients. A null-imposed dependent-wild diagnostic holds the issuer basket fixed and uses common date multipliers with three Bartlett bandwidth choices (Shao, 2010). Details and results are in Table S12. Other checks are retrospective with nominal p values. News differences use daily regressions with seven-lag Newey–West covariance estimates (Newey & West, 1987).

The curve-conditioned specification adds separate two-year level and ten-minus-two-year slope coefficients for EUR and USD on identical observations. An additional model interacts each currency-specific curve variable with the signed green-minus-conventional maturity difference, measured in years. This observable cash-flow-timing proxy allows market-rate sensitivity to vary across pairs; it is not a measured duration difference. Because rates may themselves respond to geopolitical developments, these specifications assess market sensitivity rather than identify a causal share mediated by rates. For the three documented nominal sovereign cases, conditional yields, modified durations and convexities are computed from scheduled cash flows at the 27 February quote, under both clean- and dirty-quote assumptions. These case calculations use same-day settlement and regular actual/actual coupon-period accrual; they are illustrative valuation diagnostics, not additional international regressions.

For Australia and New Zealand, daily local two- and ten-year government yields are matched to all 55 and 56 original paired observations, respectively. RBA F2 and F16 provide Australian curve and instrument yields; RBNZ B2 supplies New Zealand benchmarks and instrument yields (Reserve Bank of Australia, 2026; Reserve Bank of New Zealand, 2026). No missing rates are filled. Published yields and contractual cash flows generate alternative clean and dirty prices under same-day and two-weekday settlement assumptions. Local holiday adjustments are not modelled; regular actual/actual coupon periods are assumed. External releases can share underlying market-data inputs with LSEG, so this is a valuation-consistency check rather than an independent experiment.

### 3.4 Methodological triangulation

The design combines three evidence layers with distinct functions. First, independently dated events and sampled news establish timing and the observed information environment. Second, within-issuer paired prices and weekly estimates quantify the international market pattern. Third, contractual verification, local yield curves and external-yield price reconciliation assess the valuation interpretation. Within the third layer, a direct joint contrast tests whether the two documented cases differ conditionally; a common-date sample and alternative prices test calendar composition and price construction. These layers are complementary rather than statistically independent replications. Disagreement is retained because it distinguishes broad repricing from conditional case evidence. All checks were developed retrospectively; neither specification nor sample is selected for statistical significance.

### 4.2 Relative green bond prices

The broad-panel estimate is -0.0920 log percentage points (95% CI [-0.1828, -0.0011]; p = 0.047), approximately a 0.092% relative decline. Table 3 reports this result alongside alternative maturity, coverage and trend specifications. Among the 74 pairs, 43 have a negative pre–post change and the median change is -0.0469 log percentage points. The mean therefore describes a heterogeneous international response. The equal-pair mean is −0.0898 log percentage points, close to the fixed-effect estimate, so the negative average is not an artefact of the panel’s implicit pair weights.

The direction is stable to concentration checks. Removing one issuer at a time produces estimates between -0.1047 and -0.0764 log percentage points; removing one currency at a time gives -0.0963 and -0.0801. These are coefficient ranges, not simultaneous confidence intervals. The result is not attributable to one remaining issuer or currency, although sample and inference choices affect statistical precision. Date-score HAC results are provided separately in Table S10.

### 4.3 Comparison quality and robustness

The uniform screen classifies 35 original pairs as failing at least one observed requirement, 38 as unresolved and three as documented eligible cases. Within the retained broad panel, the corresponding counts are 33, 38 and three. The 41-pair intermediate screen excludes observed failures while retaining unresolved cases; its estimate is −0.0654 log percentage points (p=0.302). It is not a fully verified subset. The conservative any-call rule includes CPPIB’s documented tax-event redemption provision, which differs from an ordinary discretionary call and need not have a material price effect.

The documented cases are Australia, Denmark and New Zealand. Their scheduled payments and sovereign claim structures support direct cash-flow calculations. Separately, exchange metadata identify clean-price trading for both EU bonds, but the historical LSEG source/venue link is not established. Those venue records are retained as partial validation rather than used to relabel the full price panel. Denmark has identical promised coupons and maturity, alongside a one-way green-to-conventional exchange facility. Australia and New Zealand have non-identical cash flows even though they satisfy the stated eligibility tolerances. The three cases are presented individually, not pooled as an international label effect.

The contract audit identifies two distinct problems that issuer matching cannot resolve. CBA combines subordinated Tier 2 green debt with senior covered debt. Sweden combines a nominal green bond with an inflation-linked comparator; an inflation-expectations response could therefore enter their price difference. Both are excluded from the broad panel. National Grid, PacifiCorp and Intel have documented call features and are kept outside the plain-contract subset. Ireland’s official coupon schedule resolves a previously missing coupon and establishes a gap above the screen’s threshold. Instrument-level evidence and earlier estimates are retained in the supplement.

The conditional cash-flow cases show why price measurement matters economically. Australia’s unconditioned quoted-price difference is approximately −0.0227 log percentage points; adding accrued interest under a clean-quote assumption changes the valuation object and yields approximately +0.0145. Denmark’s corresponding differences remain close to −0.004, whereas New Zealand’s unconditioned differences are positive. Tables S8–S9 report these initial valuation scenarios. The subsequent local-rate analysis in Section 4.5 distinguishes these unconditional changes from conditional coefficients, and instrument-yield reconciliation provides empirical support for clean pricing in Australia and New Zealand.

A targeted coupon-calendar check excludes the EU and Polish pairs, whose scheduled payments fall within the observation period. The estimate remains −0.0843 log percentage points, retaining 91.6% of the baseline magnitude (95% CI [−0.1763, 0.0078]; p=0.072). Removing observations within five calendar days of the targeted coupon dates gives −0.0902 (p=0.052). These checks show that the two pairs do not account for most of the measured decline; they are not a complete coupon audit of the panel. Table S13 reports every targeted exclusion.

Under two-weekday settlement, clean-price RMSEs are 0.067–0.075 per 100 face value, compared with 0.803–1.328 for dirty prices across the four bonds; same-day settlement gives the same ranking (Table S16). Their joint difference is −0.0308 (p=0.020), or −0.0271 on shared dates (p=0.049). These prices are reconstructed valuations, not separately observed transactions.

The pre-event placebo gives an Australian coefficient of −0.0182 with p values of 0.021–0.038 and a New Zealand coefficient of −0.0118 with p values of 0.070–0.182. The comparison thus supports different conditional patterns in two documented pairs, rather than attributing the difference to country-level mechanisms. Case-level robustness and complete uncertainty estimates are reported in Tables S14–S16.

## 5 Discussion and implications

### 5.1 What the combined evidence adds

The three research questions yield a coherent but differentiated account. The sampled news stream becomes more negative and energy-focused, establishing the episode’s informational context. The international paired-price panel displays relative green-price weakening, providing the broad market result. The documented cases then show that the interpretation depends on local financial conditions: the Australian negative coefficient attenuates, while New Zealand has a negative conditional coefficient despite a positive unconditional estimate. The contribution lies in connecting these findings within a common valuation framework, rather than allowing the broad average to stand in for every instrument or mechanism.

The direct contrast sharpens this contribution. A significant coefficient in one regression and an insignificant coefficient in another would not establish that the coefficients differ. Equation (4) instead tests the New Zealand–Australia difference while allowing separate rate sensitivities and retaining contemporaneous covariance. Its direction persists with shared dates and yield-implied clean prices. Figure 4 also makes the changing precision visible. The comparison consequently identifies a consistent ordering in the estimated conditional changes, with uncertainty that must be evaluated across the full specification grid.

This extends, rather than overturns, the preference-based literature. Fiorillo et al. (2024) associate geopolitical risk with a stronger corporate greenium; the present negative price-change estimate concerns a different event, sample and valuation object. A security can retain a yield advantage while its price ratio declines. Similarly, the dynamics reported by Pietsch and Salakhova (2025) make demand and interest-rate conditions plausible joint influences, but do not identify which influence drives these two sovereign cases. The theoretical gain is a more precise link between environmental valuation and realised fixed-income performance: neither can be inferred reliably from the other without specifying the benchmark and conditioning set.

### 5.2 Economic magnitude and portfolio decisions

The broad change is approximately 9.2 price basis points, whereas the seven-lag original-date conditional case contrast is approximately 2.0 price basis points. These are relative-price changes, not changes in yields. Conversion into yield basis points would require instrument-specific sensitivity calculations and a stated curve-shift assumption. The smaller conditional quantities can matter for performance attribution and benchmark comparisons, but they do not establish a profitable trading strategy. The indicative cost inferred from quoted spreads exceeds the broad average change, even before funding, borrowing and exit costs.

Portfolio assessment should therefore align the economic object before evaluating success or failure. A quoted-price comparison answers a mark-to-market question. A clean-price comparison separates accrued interest, while a total-return comparison must also account for coupons and reinvestment. The cash-flow scenarios show that these distinctions can change the sign of a small difference. For the four Australian and New Zealand instruments, published-yield reconciliation favours the clean interpretation, making the conditional comparison more informative than an unsupported assumption about the entire quotation feed.

A usable benchmarking procedure follows from the evidence. First, specify the price or return objective and preserve an auditable observation calendar. Second, align issuer, currency, claim priority, indexation, optionality and payment structure before relying on maturity proximity. Third, compare baseline and market-conditioned estimates on identical rows and inspect changes in both magnitude and uncertainty. This procedure permits investors to assess sustainable mandates alongside conventional risk management without treating a green designation as a guarantee of short-run protection.

### 5.3 Implications for sovereign issuers and market infrastructure

For debt managers, transparent financial terms support the interpretation of green financing costs as well as investor due diligence. Standardised coupon, settlement, accrued-interest and option information would make comparisons more reproducible. Twin structures can reduce some contractual discrepancies, but associated exchange facilities and market liquidity still require explicit treatment. Denmark illustrates why even close cash-flow alignment does not make all institutional features identical. Issuers should distinguish environmental reporting about proceeds from the financial characteristics governing investors’ realised returns.

Recent sovereign research suggests that supply, credibility and market structure may help explain valuation heterogeneity (Ando et al., 2024; Nevatia, 2026). The working-paper evidence of Panizza et al. (2025) and Brichetti et al. (2026) further motivates attention to contractual commitments and relative supply. These are useful hypotheses for extending the present comparison, not explanations established by the observed country labels. Investor holdings, comparable liquidity measures, issuance calendars and several documented pairs per market would be needed to examine them directly.

For sustainable-finance policy, the distinction is practical: environmental eligibility establishes what can be financed, while financial resilience requires modelling rate, credit and liquidity exposure. Stress testing can include both objectives without equating them. The present evidence supports incorporating local curves and contractual cash flows into assessments of green debt during geopolitical episodes. It does not measure whether financed projects reduce emissions or improve energy independence; those outcomes require project or issuer evidence linked to the securities.

### 5.4 Evidential scope and the next empirical test

Methodological triangulation improves interpretation by confronting the market result with evidence that performs different functions. News establishes context, paired prices quantify the pattern, and contractual and valuation checks examine what that pattern represents. The layers are not three independent confirmations of a causal effect. In particular, a rate variable can be both a concurrent financial driver and a response to the geopolitical event. Conditioning cannot identify which role dominates without additional temporal or external identifying information.

The principal inferential constraints are concentrated in identifiable parts of the design: one event, a short window, retrospectively retrieved comparator candidates, incomplete contractual coverage and one detailed pair per case. The Australian pre-event placebo also discourages a simple event-break interpretation. These features do not erase the international repricing result or the conditional case comparison; they define the claims each can support. The next decisive extension would hold the verification protocol fixed, add multiple documented pairs and repeated independently dated events, and obtain liquidity and holdings measures before testing market mechanisms. That design would separate instrument variation from country variation and reduce reliance on one realised episode.

## 6 Conclusion

Green bond performance during geopolitical stress is heterogeneous even when securities share a sovereign issuer, currency and broadly comparable contractual terms. This study connects that heterogeneity to the interpretation of an international repricing episode. Its principal contribution is to distinguish the broad movement in green–conventional prices from the conditional differences observed in documented securities, and to test those differences directly. The evidence therefore advances the assessment of sustainable debt beyond a pooled claim of resilience or vulnerability: the financial benchmark, local yield curve and valuation convention determine what an observed price movement can establish.

The international evidence provides the first part of this account. Around the 28 February 2026 escalation involving Iran, the sampled news environment becomes more negative and energy-focused, while green quoted prices weaken by approximately 0.092% relative to conventional comparators across 74 issuer-matched pairs in seven currencies. Excluding individual issuers or currencies preserves the direction, and removing the two targeted coupon-payment pairs retains 91.6% of the baseline magnitude. This breadth makes the pattern economically relevant for international sustainable-debt assessment. Its interpretation changes, however, when financial conditions enter the comparison: on identical EUR/USD observations, yield-curve conditioning reduces the estimated magnitude from approximately 0.088% to 0.026%.

The documented sovereign cases provide the study’s most discriminating result. Australia’s negative coefficient attenuates toward zero after local-rate adjustment. New Zealand instead exhibits a negative conditional coefficient despite a positive unconditional estimate. Their difference is tested in a joint model that allows separate rate sensitivities and incorporates contemporaneous covariance. The estimated New Zealand–Australia contrast is approximately −0.020 log percentage points on original dates and −0.017 on shared dates. Reconstructing prices from published instrument yields preserves its negative direction. The precision varies across calendar, trend and covariance choices, but the comparison consistently places the estimated New Zealand conditional change below Australia’s. This establishes a specific comparative result without inferring country mechanisms from one pair per market.

These findings refine the relationship between sustainable-investment preferences and realised bond performance. A green yield advantage and a subsequent decline in the relative price of a green security can coexist. Accordingly, event-window price changes should not be read as direct evidence that investors have strengthened or abandoned environmental preferences. The Australian attenuation and New Zealand conditional difference demonstrate why both conventional fixed-income exposure and the remaining relative-price component deserve attention. The latter is an empirical object to explain, not automatically the price of the environmental label. Across the four case instruments, the better fit of clean-price reconstructions further anchors this interpretation in scheduled cash flows and published yields.

The practical implication is a more demanding benchmark for assessing sustainable portfolios. Investors should specify whether the objective is quoted-price performance, clean valuation or coupon-inclusive return; align financial claims before interpreting relative performance; and compare market-conditioned results on unchanged observations. Sovereign debt managers can support this process through accessible contractual schedules, settlement conventions and pricing metadata. Policymakers can assess environmental eligibility alongside conventional financial resilience, recognising that a use-of-proceeds designation does not determine duration, liquidity or protection against discount-rate changes. The measured differences are relevant to attribution and benchmarking; quoted trading costs do not support presenting them as an exploitable return opportunity.

The single-event setting, incomplete contractual coverage and small documented core define the scope of these conclusions. A larger set of verified pairs, repeated independently dated events and matched liquidity or holdings data would permit tests of the mechanisms behind the conditional differences. The present contribution is already concrete: an international repricing pattern, a directly estimated sovereign contrast and a valuation check that connects the empirical outcome to the underlying financial claims. Together, they establish that evaluating green debt during geopolitical stress requires both international market evidence and instrument-level financial analysis. Environmental designation alone is insufficient to explain the observed pattern of relative performance.
